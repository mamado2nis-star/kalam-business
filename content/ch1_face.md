# Chapter 1 — Interview · Face-to-face interview

## 1. Story Two — Face-to-face interview (p. 35–40)
Audio: ff_35

Khaled got a phone call for the panel interview. He is being interviewed for an electronics engineer job by Rehab Osama, who is in charge of recruiting for the company, and two of her colleagues: Mazen, an engineering manager, and Mona, the new HR manager.

Rehab: Good morning, Khaled. Thanks for coming in. I'm Rehab Osama, the recruiting lead.
Khaled: Good morning, Ms. Rehab. Thanks for having me.
Rehab: Let me introduce my colleagues. To my left is Mazen, engineering manager for the printers department.
Khaled: Hello, Mr. Mazen.
Rehab: And next to him is Mona from HR. Mona will be sitting in for Susan, who we spoke to on the phone.
Khaled: Good morning.
Mona: Morning.
Rehab: Before we start, I'd like to go over the ground rules for today, just to give you an idea of the format.
Khaled: OK, certainly.
Rehab: I'll be leading the meeting, covering most of the basic questions. Mona will mainly observe, but she'll join in if she wants to clarify anything. Mazen will handle the more technical aspects.
Khaled: Good to hear. Do you mind if I take notes while we're speaking? I'd like to keep track of things.
Rehab: There's no issue at all. So, let's get started. I see from your résumé that you went to Cairo University for electronics engineering. What else can you tell us about yourself?
Khaled: Well, I've always been very interested in how things are wired and how devices function. I like to know how things work — or, if they don't work, I want to know why. I really enjoy analyzing situations and solving problems.
Mazen: Great. Let's talk about your coursework. What in particular did you cover that you think is relevant to this position?
Khaled: A lot of courses from my core curriculum come to mind. Circuits and signal processing were my favorites, and I was able to excel in those subjects. I also did well in optimization and linear algebra, along with all my electronics engineering courses. And I think the work we did in process analysis would be a great help, as it's such a key skill for any project.
Mazen: Those are pretty typical courses. Anything else that stands out?
Khaled: Cairo University places great emphasis on thorough testing and project-management processes — I think more than other colleges. The electrical engineering program is really oriented toward real-life situations.
Rehab: So, let's talk about experience. Did you take part in any extracurricular activities that relate to this job?
Khaled: Yes. I was on the college robotics team for two years. Just getting on the team is a competition. I was fortunate to compete in two or three national championships.
Mazen: OK, but how would that make you a good test engineer?
Khaled: Well, of course, we were constantly testing our robots, and when something went wrong, my teammates looked to me to figure it out. One time we couldn't get some parts, so it was up to me to scavenge parts and adapt them to our needs.
Mazen: That takes a good bit of patience and creativity, I bet. What do you find to be the most rewarding part of working as an electrical engineer?
Khaled: Right. For me, seeing a project go from an idea to a fully functioning device is the most rewarding part of the job. I take great pride in the work I do. Knowing that the technology I helped design and develop can directly improve people's personal and professional lives makes this work even more rewarding.
Rehab: Beautiful. That leads me to my next question, then. We see a lot of candidates with impressive grades and internships. What makes you different?
Khaled: I'm open to new things. For me, it goes beyond academics. Here's an example: like a lot of students, I took a gap year, but I wanted to use this time as an opportunity.
Rehab: So what did you do?
Khaled: I went to work for my uncle, who runs an electronics repair shop in downtown Cairo. We did small repairs for individuals, in the shop and in their homes, and I did set up a couple of networks for small businesses. The experience taught me more about how people use products, and it helped me put faces on the people who are our end users.
Rehab: That's interesting — and frankly, something more companies should be aware of. Tell me why you are the right person for this job.
Khaled: I'm very creative, and I know a lot about how electronic circuits work and how to design them. I would help your company keep its reputation for quality by paying close attention to the little things. And there's something else I can bring to the table: the customer-relations side of things.
Rehab: So let's talk about that. What is it that attracts you to the position?
Khaled: I am a big fan of your company. I follow your work, and I can see your impact on the electronics industry, and that actually inspired me to apply for the job. I love working with a team to achieve a common goal, and I look forward to becoming a valued contributor to this phenomenal team.
Mona: Right. Most people have their own personal career goals as well. What are your long-term objectives?
Khaled: Well, I have a broad outlook. I definitely plan to stay in the electronics field. It's always been my passion, and I plan to keep learning, especially since technology changes every day.
Mona: OK, but can you be more specific?
Khaled: Well, I could see myself in a supervisory role, coordinating with other test engineers, developers and vendors. I'm an effective troubleshooter and I like taking care of details, but I always keep an eye on the big picture — and it all comes down to end users and making sure they have a positive experience with the company.
Rehab: Great. Well, I think it's time to start wrapping up. Mazen, Mona, do you have anything to add?
Mona: No, I think we're all set.
Rehab: All right. Thanks for coming in, Khaled. We'll finish reviewing all the candidates in the next couple of days. If you're selected for the second round, we'll notify you by email.
Khaled: Thank you, Ms. Rehab, and thank you, Ms. Mona and Mr. Mazen, for your time.

### Speaking about your internship — important phrases
I completed my internship as a ___ at ___.
I have two internships under my belt.

### Top soft skills you learn during an internship
Communication and interpersonal skills: listening, speaking, observing and empathizing. It is also helpful to understand the differences between face-to-face, phone and digital communication.
Teamwork and collaboration: the qualities that allow you to work well with others during conversations, projects and meetings.
Time management: the ability to use your time effectively, including prioritizing, planning, scheduling and organizing tasks.
Self-awareness: the ability to honestly perceive your own feelings, attitudes and reactions, without anxiety.
Critical thinking: the ability to think clearly and logically and see how ideas fit together.
Accountability: taking ownership of your actions and being responsible for your own and your team's results in the workplace.

### Sample answer
I completed my internship at ___ for four months. The senior employees there were very helpful; they helped me with my work and taught me about the field I'm going to work in. I learned a lot during my internship. For instance, I learned how to use my academic knowledge for real-time applications and how companies actually work on projects. I learned how to work in groups, think critically, and communicate and collaborate effectively. I've also learned how to take ownership of my actions and be responsible for my team's results in the workplace, not just my own.

## 6. Speaking about your strengths and weaknesses (p. 59–66)

### Strengths
Time management and leadership skills: I would say my strengths include good time management and the ability to lead a team. Since I was young, I've developed the habit of listing all my tasks and allocating time to each one. This keeps me on track and helps me finish all my tasks on time. I feel this strength would come in handy for this role, as it can help me meet deadlines with ease. I also have experience leading teams during my college years, which increased my knowledge of the challenges a leader faces and how to keep the team united to achieve the required goals.
Quick learner: I would say my greatest strength is my ability to pick things up very quickly. I am a fast learner and very keen to acquire new skills. This means I have always been very willing to take on extra responsibilities, as I see them as an opportunity to learn more. Throughout university, I took on many additional roles, including becoming a mentor and volunteering at various open days and events on campus. This helped build my confidence and strengthen my communication and time-management skills.
Passion for work: My biggest strength is probably my passion for work and my motivation to learn and grow professionally. Everything is new for me right now; I feel excited, ready to learn and do my best. If we talk about my character, I'd point out a strong sense of responsibility and an ability to work in teams. I hope I can use my strengths in your company.
Communication skills: I consider my communication skills and understanding of other people my greatest strengths. I read a lot of books on human psychology and customer behavior and tried to learn how to connect with all kinds of people. My friends would say I'm intelligent and a good companion for any conversation. Still, I know there is room to improve, and I continue studying and practicing to become even better.
Empathy with customer service: My greatest strength is that I'm a kind and empathetic person. I'm skilled at listening to people and making them feel heard. At my internship over the summer, I was working the support line and received a call from a disgruntled customer who had been dropped from our service. While the company couldn't find a solution for her, I walked her through other options she had, so she came away with a positive interaction with the company. I know the importance of a happy customer, and I'm willing to remain upbeat and solutions-oriented.
Work ethic: My strongest asset is my work ethic and my willingness to step in when needed. I'm not afraid to take on a difficult client or a project nobody else wants, because those are the ones that teach me the most. I typically love to work outside my job description and do whatever is needed. I take great pride in my ability to step in and adapt to any situation to get the best results for the company. I'm also committed to meeting deadlines and completing all tasks to high standards.
Positive attitude: One quality that makes me a great team member is my positive attitude. In both personal and professional interactions, I think it's important to always approach things with a positive mindset — whether it's getting a positive response from a difficult client or putting out a fire in a chaotic situation. Keeping things upbeat and productive is something I do well.

### Weaknesses
Self-criticism: I can be quite critical of myself, which can lead to negative self-talk and eventual burnout. I've found that I can avoid this by recording my goals, objectives and key results, and setting aside time to celebrate milestones and achievements, big and small.
Procrastination: Procrastination has long been a bad habit of mine; to be honest, I think it stems from a fear of failure. In my last job as a real-estate agent, keeping up with appointments and critical paperwork was essential to success. I started using Google Calendar and apps like Trello to manage my time better. Crossing things off my to-do list makes me feel accomplished, and I've learned to tackle harder tasks early in the day, when I'm refreshed and less likely to put them off.
Difficulty asking questions: I tend to believe I can solve any problem on my own. This works well in some situations, but in many cases I need the help of others to overcome factors beyond my control. I've been training myself to take a step back before diving into problem-solving mode and identify people or groups who can be resources for me. After all, the best way to learn is by asking questions.
Difficulty saying no: I consider myself pretty good at multitasking, and I like to stay busy. I also don't like to say no, because I feel as though I'm not living up to my potential. At my previous part-time job, I frequently worked on several tasks at a time, often assigned by other members of my team, not just my supervisor, and I took on the challenge of completing them all before my shift was over. This became extremely overwhelming, and sometimes it affected the quality of my work. I've learned that it's OK to say no. You don't have to be a superhero; we're only human and can only do so much.
Perfectionism: I have always been a perfectionist, finding ways to improve something until I'm happy with it, even if I sometimes go all out and overdo it. My friends always pointed out this habit, and I only realized it a few years ago during a college project, when my need to strive for perfection almost cost me the credit for that course. I learned my lesson and have always tried to finish things within the time available, giving my best and staying content with the results.
Lack of experience: I think one of my main weaknesses at this point in my career is my lack of experience. I've only been working in the field for two years, so I realize I have limited experience and still have a lot to learn. Though I am a quick learner and always eager to challenge myself, I know that building expertise takes time. I am looking forward to continuing my career journey and learning from colleagues who have more experience than me.
Not good at public speaking: I get nervous about addressing large groups. I feel comfortable speaking up in team meetings and giving presentations to my co-workers, but in a more formal setting or in front of a crowd of strangers, I do struggle. I don't want this to hold me back, so I'm trying to build my confidence. A friend recommended a public-speaking course, so I signed up for it to improve this important skill.
